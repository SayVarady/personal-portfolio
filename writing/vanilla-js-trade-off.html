Vanilla JS Over a Framework: A Trade-off I Keep Revisiting

Decision Trade-off

I write Vue every day at work. Components, reactivity, the whole ecosystem — it's second nature at this point. So the obvious choice for a personal portfolio was Vue too. I didn't do that, and I still go back and forth on whether it was right.

The Decision

This site is plain HTML, CSS, and vanilla JavaScript — no build step, no framework, no dependencies beyond a Google font. The reasoning: a portfolio's job is to load instantly, deploy anywhere without a CI pipeline, and stay readable to anyone who opens the source — including non-Vue developers evaluating whether to hire me. A build pipeline for a handful of static sections felt like solving a problem I didn't have.

The Rebuild

The first version leaned into that freedom in the wrong direction — a custom cursor, a canvas full of drifting sakura petals, a typed-text role cycler, animated skill bars, glass-card panels, a contact form that faked a "message sent" state. None of it was hard to build by hand, which was the problem. It was easy to keep adding, and every addition made the site read less like mine and more like a template built to impress a screenshot.

So I stripped it back to a single typographic column: one accent color, hairline rules instead of cards, no animation that isn't load-bearing. What's left is closer to what I actually wanted to say — here's who I am, here's what I've built, here's how to reach me — without the effects doing the talking for me.

The Trade-off

Vanilla JS still costs what it always cost: no reactive state, no component boundaries, a handful of small IIFEs in main.js instead of a framework doing it for me. That's fine at this size — a nav toggle, an active-link highlight, one honest counter for years of experience. If this site ever grows into something with real state — a CMS-backed writing index, saved drafts, anything stateful across pages — that's the point I'd reach for Vue instead of pretending the trade-off doesn't exist.

Restraint isn't the absence of effort. Cutting the cursor trail took longer than building it did.
